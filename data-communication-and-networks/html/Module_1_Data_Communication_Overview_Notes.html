Topic 13: Channel Capacity Theorems [UPLOADED PYQ]

1. Nyquist Bit Rate (For Noiseless Channels): $$C = 2 \times B \times \log_2(L) \quad \text{bps}$$ Where $B$ is channel bandwidth in Hertz ($\text{Hz}$) and $L$ is number of discrete signal voltage levels.
2. Shannon Channel Capacity (For Noisy Channels with Thermal Noise): $$C = B \times \log_2(1 + \text{SNR}) \quad \text{bps}$$ Where $\text{SNR} = 10^{\frac{\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}}}{10}}$ is the linear Signal-to-Noise Ratio.
🏛️ [UPLOADED PYQ] Numerical: Shannon Capacity for Bandwidth $4\text{ kHz}$ and $\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 24\text{ dB}$
  1. Linear $\text{SNR} = 10^{\frac{24}{10}} = 10^{2.4} \approx \mathbf{251.19}$.
  2. $C = 4000 \times \log_2(1 + 251.19) = 4000 \times \log_2(252.19) \approx 4000 \times 7.978 = \mathbf{31,913} \text{ bps} \ (\approx 32 \text{ kbps})$.

🧠 M1 Active Recall & Exam Questions [UPLOADED PYQ]

Q1. [UPLOADED PYQ] Compare OSI and TCP/IP models across 5 engineering parameters. (8 Marks)
1. Layer Count: OSI has 7 layers; TCP/IP has 4 (or 5) layers.
2. Origins: OSI was designed by ISO as a formal conceptual reference model before protocols were written; TCP/IP was designed pragmatically around actual working protocols (ARPANET).
3. Services vs Protocols: OSI strictly separates Services, Interfaces, and Protocols; TCP/IP combines services into protocols directly.
4. Network Layer Communication: OSI supports both Connectionless and Connection-Oriented at Network layer; TCP/IP supports only Connectionless (IP).
5. Transport Layer Communication: OSI supports only Connection-Oriented; TCP/IP supports both Connection-Oriented (TCP) and Connectionless (UDP).

🏛️ Solved Numerical 1: Decibel Loss and Multistage Repeater Amplification

A signal travels through a transmission line consisting of three consecutive cable sections and two intermediate in-line amplifiers:

  • Cable Section 1: Loss of $-12\text{ dB}$
  • Amplifier 1: Gain of $+35\text{ dB}$
  • Cable Section 2: Loss of $-18\text{ dB}$
  • Amplifier 2: Gain of $+20\text{ dB}$
  • Cable Section 3: Loss of $-15\text{ dB}$

If the input signal power is $P_{\text{in}} = 2\text{ mW}$, calculate: (1) Total net gain/loss in dB, and (2) Final output power $P_{\text{out}}$.

Solution:

$$\text{Total Net dB} = (-12) + (+35) + (-18) + (+20) + (-15) = \mathbf{+10 \text{ dB (Net Amplification)}}$$ $$\text{Net dB} = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}\right) \implies 10 = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{2\text{ mW}}\right)$$ $$\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{2\text{ mW}}\right) = 1 \implies \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{2\text{ mW}} = 10^1 = 10 \implies \mathbf{P_{\text{out}} = 20 \text{ mW}}$$
🏛️ Solved Numerical 2: Satellite Link Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP)

A 100 Mbps satellite link has a propagation delay of 250 ms. Calculate the Bandwidth-Delay Product in bits and bytes.

Solution:

$$\text{BDP} = \text{Bandwidth} \times \text{Round-Trip Time (RTT)} = 100 \times 10^6 \text{ bps} \times (2 \times 0.250\text{ s})$$ $$\text{BDP} = 100 \times 10^6 \times 0.500\text{ s} = \mathbf{50,000,000 \text{ bits}} = \mathbf{6,250,000 \text{ bytes (6.25 MB)}}$$

Interpretation: The sender can transmit 6.25 MB of data before receiving the first ACK acknowledgement!

🏛️ Solved Numerical 3: Multi-Level Signaling Nyquist Limit

A digital transmission system must transmit 64 kbps over a channel with bandwidth 4 kHz. What is the minimum Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) in dB required to support this data rate according to Shannon's Theorem?

Solution:

$$C = B \log_2(1 + \text{SNR}) \implies 64,000 = 4000 \times \log_2(1 + \text{SNR})$$ $$\log_2(1 + \text{SNR}) = \frac{64,000}{4000} = 16 \implies 1 + \text{SNR} = 2^{16} = 65,536 \implies \text{SNR} = 65,535$$ $$\mathbf{\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10 \log_{10}(65,535) \approx 10 \times 4.8165 = \mathbf{48.17 \text{ dB}}}$$

Topic 13.3: In-Depth Theoretical Foundations of Data Transmission

A physical transmission medium acts as a Bandpass Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) Filter, attenuating high-frequency harmonics and causing phase shifts. In electromagnetic wave theory, signal propagation velocity through dielectric media is given by:

Electromagnetic Propagation Velocity & Wavelength: $$\mathbf{v = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}} = \frac{3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}}{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}}}$$ $$\mathbf{\lambda = \frac{v}{f}}$$ Where $\epsilon_r$ is the relative dielectric constant of the insulating medium ($\epsilon_r \approx 2.1\text{ to }2.3$ for solid Teflon/Polyethylene in coaxial and twisted pair cables, yielding $v \approx 2 \times 10^8\text{ m/s} = \frac{2}{3} c$).
🏛️ Step-by-Step Solved Problem: Multistage Communication Link Budget & SNR Cascades

An optical fiber transmission link spans $L = 80\text{ km}$. The optical transmitter launches an optical power of $P_{\text{tx}} = 5\text{ mW}$ ($+7\text{ dBm}$) at $\lambda = 1550\text{ nm}$. The fiber cable exhibits an attenuation coefficient of $\alpha = 0.25\text{ dB/km}$. The link contains 10 optical fusion splices with loss $0.1\text{ dB/splice}$ and 2 optical connectors with loss $0.5\text{ dB/connector}$. If the optical receiver has a minimum sensitivity of $-25\text{ dBm}$, calculate the optical power margin (safety margin).

Solution:

  • Total Fiber Cable Loss: $\alpha \times L = 0.25\text{ dB/km} \times 80\text{ km} = \mathbf{20.0 \text{ dB}}$
  • Total Splice Loss: $10 \times 0.1\text{ dB} = \mathbf{1.0 \text{ dB}}$
  • Total Connector Loss: $2 \times 0.5\text{ dB} = \mathbf{1.0 \text{ dB}}$
  • Total Link Channel Attenuation: $20.0 + 1.0 + 1.0 = \mathbf{22.0 \text{ dB}}$
  • Received Optical Power ($P_{\text{rx}}$): $P_{\text{tx}} - \text{Total Loss} = +7\text{ dBm} - 22.0\text{ dB} = \mathbf{-15.0 \text{ dBm}}$
  • Optical Power Margin: $P_{\text{rx}} - \text{Receiver Sensitivity} = -15.0\text{ dBm} - (-25.0\text{ dBm}) = \mathbf{+10.0 \text{ dB}}$

Conclusion: The link possesses a robust $10\text{ dB}$ optical power margin, ensuring high operational reliability against laser aging and component degradation!

🏛️ Solved Numerical 4: Nyquist Multi-Level Constellation Design

A TV broadcast channel has a bandwidth of $B = 6\text{ MHz}$. We wish to transmit a digital HDTV stream at a data rate of $R = 30\text{ Mbps}$ over this noiseless channel.

  1. What is the minimum number of discrete signal voltage levels ($L$) required by the signaling hardware?
  2. If each discrete level is represented by $k$ bits, what is the value of $k$?

Solution:

$$R = 2 B \log_2(L) \implies 30 \times 10^6 = 2 \times (6 \times 10^6) \times \log_2(L)$$ $$\log_2(L) = \frac{30}{12} = 2.5 \implies L = 2^{2.5} \approx 5.657$$

Since the number of physical signal levels $L$ must be an integer power of 2 ($2^k$), we round up to the next power of 2:

$$\mathbf{k = 3 \text{ bits/symbol} \implies L = 2^3 = \mathbf{8 \text{ signal levels}}}$$

Actual Max Bit Rate with $L=8$: $R_{\text{actual}} = 2 \times 6\text{ MHz} \times 3 = \mathbf{36 \text{ Mbps}}$ (exceeds the required $30\text{ Mbps}$).

🏛️ Solved Numerical 5: Shannon Limit on Deep Space Satellite Channel

A deep space satellite probe transmits telemetry data over a microwave radio channel with bandwidth $B = 10\text{ MHz}$ and Signal-to-Noise Ratio $\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = -3\text{ dB}$ (noise power exceeds signal power!). Calculate the maximum theoretical bit rate.

Solution:

$$\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = -3\text{ dB} \implies -3 = 10 \log_{10}(\text{SNR}) \implies \text{SNR} = 10^{-0.3} \approx 0.5012$$ $$\mathbf{C = B \log_2(1 + \text{SNR}) = 10 \times 10^6 \times \log_2(1 + 0.5012) = 10^7 \times \log_2(1.5012)}$$ $$\text{Since } \log_2(1.5012) \approx 0.5861: \quad \mathbf{C = 10^7 \times 0.5861 = \mathbf{5,861,000 \text{ bps} \approx \mathbf{5.86 \text{ Mbps}}}}$$

Significance: Shannon's theorem proves that reliable, error-free communication is mathematically possible even when noise is greater than signal power ($\text{SNR} < 1$), provided robust error-correcting codes are utilized!

🏛️ Solved Numerical 6: End-to-End Packet Delay Calculation (Transmission, Propagation, Queuing, Processing)

A host transmits a packet of size $L = 1500\text{ bytes}$ across a path of $N = 4$ identical communication links. Each link has a transmission bandwidth of $R = 10\text{ Mbps}$, a physical length of $D = 250\text{ km}$, and propagation speed $v = 2 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$. Each intermediate router introduces an average processing delay of $d_{\text{proc}} = 10 \ \mu\text{s}$ and queuing delay of $d_{\text{queue}} = 40 \ \mu\text{s}$. Calculate the total end-to-end latency from source to destination.

Solution: Total Latency $T_{\text{total}} = N \times (T_{\text{trans}} + T_{\text{prop}}) + (N - 1) \times (d_{\text{proc}} + d_{\text{queue}})$.

  • Transmission Delay per link: $T_{\text{trans}} = \frac{1500 \times 8 \text{ bits}}{10 \times 10^6 \text{ bps}} = \frac{12,000}{10^7} = 0.0012\text{ s} = \mathbf{1.20 \text{ ms}}$.
  • Propagation Delay per link: $T_{\text{prop}} = \frac{250 \times 10^3 \text{ m}}{2 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}} = 0.00125\text{ s} = \mathbf{1.25 \text{ ms}}$.
  • Nodal Delay per intermediate router: $d_{\text{nodal}} = 10 \ \mu\text{s} + 40 \ \mu\text{s} = 50 \ \mu\text{s} = \mathbf{0.05 \text{ ms}}$.
  • There are $N=4$ links and $(N-1) = 3$ intermediate routers:
    • $$\mathbf{T_{\text{total}} = 4 \times (1.20\text{ ms} + 1.25\text{ ms}) + 3 \times (0.05\text{ ms}) = 4 \times 2.45\text{ ms} + 0.15\text{ ms} = 9.80\text{ ms} + 0.15\text{ ms} = \mathbf{9.95 \text{ ms}}}$$

Topic 13.7: Exhaustive Signal Analysis & Fourier Representation

According to Fourier Analysis, any composite analog periodic signal can be decomposed into an infinite sum of simple sine and cosine waves (harmonics): $$x(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left[ a_n \cos(2\pi n f_0 t) + b_n \sin(2\pi n f_0 t) \right]$$ Where $f_0 = 1/T$ is the fundamental harmonic frequency. When a square wave (representing digital binary pulses) is transmitted over a physical channel with finite bandwidth $B$, the channel filters out high-frequency harmonics, rounding the sharp rectangular edges.

🏛️ Solved Problem: Harmonic Bandwidth Requirements for Square Waves

A digital system transmits a square wave clock signal at fundamental frequency $f_0 = 1\text{ MHz}$ ($1\text{ Mbps}$ rate for alternating `0101`).

  • To transmit fundamental only: Bandwidth $B = 1\text{ MHz}$ $\implies$ Approximate sine wave shape.
  • To transmit up to 3rd harmonic ($3\text{ MHz}$): Bandwidth $B = 3\text{ MHz}$ $\implies$ Noticeable squaring of corners.
  • To transmit up to 5th harmonic ($5\text{ MHz}$): Bandwidth $B = 5\text{ MHz}$ $\implies$ Sharp, reliable digital pulse edges!

Standard Telecom Rule: A transmission channel must pass at least up to the 5th harmonic ($B \ge 5 f_0$) to enable reliable, low-jitter threshold detection at the receiver!

🏛️ Solved Numerical 7: Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) in Cascaded Amplifiers (Friis Formula)

Two amplifiers are connected in cascade: Amplifier 1 has Gain $G_1 = 10\text{ dB}$ ($G_1 = 10$) and Noise Figure $F_1 = 3\text{ dB}$ ($F_1 = 2$). Amplifier 2 has Gain $G_2 = 20\text{ dB}$ ($G_2 = 100$) and Noise Figure $F_2 = 6\text{ dB}$ ($F_2 = 4$). Calculate the total system Noise Figure ($F_{\text{total}}$) using the Friis Formula.

$$\mathbf{F_{\text{total}} = F_1 + \frac{F_2 - 1}{G_1} = 2 + \frac{4 - 1}{10} = 2 + 0.3 = \mathbf{2.3}}$$ $$\mathbf{F_{\text{total(dB)}} = 10 \log_{10}(2.3) \approx \mathbf{3.62 \text{ dB}}}$$

Design Insight: The noise figure of the first amplifier dominates the overall receiver noise performance, proving that low-noise pre-amplifiers (LNAs) must always be placed at the very front of the antenna receiver chain!

Q13. Explain the detailed structure of the Global Internet Hierarchy: Tier-1, Tier-2, Tier-3 ISPs, Internet Exchange Points (IXPs), and Peering vs Transit agreements. (10 Marks)
The Internet is a global hierarchical network of networks:
• Tier-1 ISPs (National/Global Backbones): AT&T, Lumen, Telia, Tata Communications, NTT. They own intercontinental fiber optic cables and peer with all other Tier-1 providers settlement-free (zero transit fees).
• Tier-2 ISPs (Regional Providers): Purchase transit from Tier-1 ISPs to reach the global Internet, while peering with other Tier-2 ISPs locally.
• Tier-3 ISPs (Local Access ISPs): Comcast, Jio, Airtel, local cable providers connecting end-user residential homes and enterprise campuses.
• Internet Exchange Points (IXPs): Physical data center facilities where multiple ISPs and CDNs (Google, Cloudflare, Akamai) interconnect via high-speed switching fabrics to exchange traffic locally without incurring transit costs.
• Peering vs. Transit: Peering is a bilateral agreement to exchange mutual customer traffic for free; Transit is a commercial contract where a customer ISP pays an upstream provider for complete global Internet routing access.

🏛️ Step-by-Step Solved Problem: Satellite Link Up-Link and Down-Link Frequency Planning

Geostationary communication satellites operate in paired frequency bands where the Up-Link frequency ($f_{\text{up}}$) is intentionally higher than the Down-Link frequency ($f_{\text{down}}$):

  • C-Band: Up-Link $= 5.925\text{--}6.425\text{ GHz}$ ($6\text{ GHz}$); Down-Link $= 3.700\text{--}4.200\text{ GHz}$ ($4\text{ GHz}$).
  • Ku-Band: Up-Link $= 14.0\text{--}14.5\text{ GHz}$ ($14\text{ GHz}$); Down-Link $= 11.7\text{--}12.2\text{ GHz}$ ($12\text{ GHz}$).
  • Ka-Band: Up-Link $= 27.5\text{--}31.0\text{ GHz}$ ($30\text{ GHz}$); Down-Link $= 17.7\text{--}21.2\text{ GHz}$ ($20\text{ GHz}$).

Physical Rationale: Higher frequencies suffer higher free-space path loss ($L_{\text{path}} \propto f^2$). Ground earth stations have access to virtually unlimited electrical power grids to drive high-wattage Traveling Wave Tube Amplifiers (TWTAs) for the higher up-link frequency, whereas satellite transponders are strictly constrained by onboard solar panel energy budgets, necessitating the lower frequency for the down-link!

Topic 13.10: Advanced Signal Propagation & Multipath Fading Models

In terrestrial and cellular wireless networks, electromagnetic radio signals travel from transmitter to receiver across multiple physical paths via reflection from buildings, diffraction around obstacles, and scattering from foliage.

Free-Space Path Loss (Friis Transmission Equation): $$\mathbf{P_r = P_t \cdot G_t \cdot G_r \cdot \left(\frac{\lambda}{4 \pi d}\right)^2 = P_t \cdot G_t \cdot G_r \cdot \left(\frac{c}{4 \pi d f}\right)^2}$$ $$\mathbf{\text{Path Loss (dB)} = 20 \log_{10}(d) + 20 \log_{10}(f) + 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{4\pi}{c}\right) - G_{t\text{(dBi)}} - G_{r\text{(dBi)}}}$$ Where $d$ is the distance between antennas, $f$ is the carrier frequency, $G_t, G_r$ are transmitter and receiver antenna gains.
Fading Phenomenon Physical Mechanism Mitigation Strategy
1. Large-Scale Path Loss Signal power decays exponentially with distance ($P_r \propto 1/d^n$ where $n = 2\text{ to }4$). Cellular base station micro-cell sectorization & power control.
2. Shadowing (Log-Normal) Obstruction of line-of-sight path by large physical structures (hills, skyscrapers). Macro-diversity (handover between multiple base stations).
3. Small-Scale Rayleigh Fading Destructive interference between multiple arriving signal paths shifting over fractions of a wavelength. MIMO spatial diversity, RAKE receivers in CDMA, OFDM subcarriers.
4. Frequency-Selective Fading Coherence bandwidth of channel is smaller than transmitted signal bandwidth ($B_c < B_s$). Adaptive equalization and Cyclic-Prefix OFDM modulation.
🏛️ Solved Numerical 8: Free-Space Path Loss Calculation for 5G Millimeter Wave Link

A 5G NR base station transmits at frequency $f = 28\text{ GHz}$ ($\lambda = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{28 \times 10^9} = 0.0107\text{ m} = 10.7\text{ mm}$) to a user equipment (UE) located $d = 500\text{ meters}$ away. Transmitter antenna gain is $G_t = 15\text{ dBi}$, and receiver antenna gain is $G_r = 5\text{ dBi}$. Calculate the free-space path loss in dB.

Solution:

$$\text{Path Loss} = 20 \log_{10}(500) + 20 \log_{10}(28 \times 10^9) + 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{4\pi}{3 \times 10^8}\right) - 15 - 5$$ $$\text{Path Loss} = 53.98 + 208.94 - 147.56 - 20 = \mathbf{95.36 \text{ dB}}$$

Conclusion: High path loss ($>95\text{ dB}$ over $500\text{ m}$) necessitates high-gain beamforming phased array antennas in 5G mmWave base stations!

🏛️ Solved Numerical 9: Packetization Delay vs. Propagation Latency in Real-Time Voice (VoIP)

In a Voice-over-IP (VoIP) network, human speech is encoded at $64\text{ kbps}$ using G.711 PCM. The voice encoder generates $20\text{ ms}$ voice sample frames before encapsulating into RTP/UDP/IP packets ($40\text{ bytes}$ header). The packet travels across a $2000\text{ km}$ fiber optic link ($v = 2 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$) with transmission bandwidth $100\text{ Mbps}$. Calculate: (1) Payload size per VoIP packet in bytes, (2) Packetization delay, (3) Transmission delay, (4) Propagation delay, and (5) Total one-way latency.

Solution:

  • Payload size per $20\text{ ms}$ voice block: $\text{Payload} = \frac{64,000 \text{ bps} \times 0.020\text{ s}}{8 \text{ bits/byte}} = \mathbf{160 \text{ bytes}}$.
  • Total Packet Size (Header + Data): $40 + 160 = \mathbf{200 \text{ bytes (1600 bits)}}$.
  • Packetization Delay: The time required to accumulate $20\text{ ms}$ of continuous human speech = $\mathbf{20.0 \text{ ms}}$.
  • Transmission Delay: $T_{\text{trans}} = \frac{1600 \text{ bits}}{100 \times 10^6 \text{ bps}} = 0.000016\text{ s} = \mathbf{0.016 \text{ ms}}$.
  • Propagation Delay: $T_{\text{prop}} = \frac{2000 \times 10^3 \text{ m}}{2 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}} = 0.010\text{ s} = \mathbf{10.0 \text{ ms}}$.
  • Total One-Way Voice Latency: $20.0\text{ ms} + 0.016\text{ ms} + 10.0\text{ ms} = \mathbf{30.016 \text{ ms}}$ (Well within ITU-T G.114 target of $< 150\text{ ms}$ for crystal-clear interactive voice!).
Q15. Explain Network Reliability, Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF), and Mean Time to Repair (MTTR) with Availability formula. (6 Marks)
Network Availability measures the percentage of operational uptime in a communication infrastructure:
$$\mathbf{\text{Availability } A = \frac{\text{MTBF}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}} \times 100\%}$$ Where $\text{MTBF}$ is Mean Time Between Failures, and $\text{MTTR}$ is Mean Time to Repair. A telecom-grade "Five Nines" ($99.999\%$) network allows no more than 5.26 minutes of unscheduled downtime per year, necessitating redundant hot-standby routers, dual-homed BGP links, and rapid failover protocols!

Topic 13.12: Master Solved Numerical on Shannon Capacity & Eb/N0

🏛️ Solved Numerical 10: Energy-per-Bit to Noise Power Spectral Density Ratio ($E_b/N_0$)

In digital communications, Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) is related to the normalized parameter $\frac{E_b}{N_0}$ (energy per bit to noise power spectral density) by:

$$\mathbf{\frac{E_b}{N_0} = \text{SNR} \times \frac{B}{R}}$$

Where $B$ is channel bandwidth in Hz, and $R$ is bit rate in bps. If a satellite channel requires a bit rate $R = 2\text{ Mbps}$ over bandwidth $B = 1\text{ MHz}$ with $\text{SNR} = 15$ ($11.76\text{ dB}$):

$$\mathbf{\frac{E_b}{N_0} = 15 \times \frac{10^6 \text{ Hz}}{2 \times 10^6 \text{ bps}} = 15 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{7.5}}$$ $$\mathbf{\left(\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right)_{\text{dB}} = 10 \log_{10}(7.5) \approx \mathbf{8.75 \text{ dB}}}$$

Shannon's Ultimate Limit: As bandwidth $B \rightarrow \infty$, the minimum theoretical threshold for error-free transmission is the famous Shannon Limit:

$$\mathbf{\left(\frac{E_b}{N_0}\right)_{\text{min}} = \ln(2) \approx 0.693 = \mathbf{-1.59 \text{ dB}}}$$
Q16. Explain Baud Rate (Modulation Rate) vs. Bit Rate (Data Rate) with formula and modulation examples. (6 Marks)
• Bit Rate ($R$): The number of binary bits ($0, 1$) transmitted per second (bps).
• Baud Rate ($S$): The number of distinct signal units (symbols) transmitted per second (baud).
$$\mathbf{R = S \times r = S \times \log_2(L)}$$ Where $L$ is the number of constellation points/levels and $r = \log_2(L)$ is bits/symbol. In 256-QAM ($L=256 \implies r=8$), a baud rate of $S = 1000\text{ baud}$ produces a bit rate of $R = 8000\text{ bps}$!
