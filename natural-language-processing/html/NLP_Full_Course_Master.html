Topic 1 & 2: Introduction to NLP & The Fundamental Ambiguity Challenge [UPLOADED PYQ]

Natural Language Processing (NLP) sits at the intersection of Artificial Intelligence and Computational Linguistics, pursuing two fundamental objectives:

  • Scientific Goal: Formulating computational models of human cognitive language understanding and production.
  • Engineering Goal: Developing robust, scalable software systems capable of processing human text and speech for practical real-world utility.
[UPLOADED PYQ — CS633] Why is Ambiguity the Core Challenge of NLP?
Natural language is inherently ambiguous across all linguistic tiers:
  1. Lexical Ambiguity: A single word possesses multiple distinct meanings (e.g., `"bank"` $\rightarrow$ financial institution vs. river slope).
  2. Syntactic (Structural) Ambiguity: A sentence can generate multiple valid parse trees (e.g., "I saw the man with the telescope" $\rightarrow$ who holds the telescope?).
  3. Semantic Ambiguity: A sentence has multiple literal interpretations (e.g., "The chicken is ready to eat" $\rightarrow$ is the chicken hungry or cooked?).
  4. Pragmatic Ambiguity: Meaning depends strictly on conversational context and real-world intent (e.g., "Can you pass the salt?" $\rightarrow$ an action request, not an inquiry about physical ability).

Topic 3 – 6: The 4 Core Levels of Linguistic Analysis

Linguistic Level Core Scope & Analysis Mechanism Representative Example
1. Morphology Study of the internal structure of words and their minimal meaning-bearing units (Morphemes).
• Free: Can stand alone (`"happy"`).
• Bound: Prefixes/Suffixes (`"un-"`, `"-ness"`).
• Inflectional: Changes grammar form (`"walk"` $\rightarrow$ `"walked"`).
• Derivational: Creates new words/parts of speech (`"happy"` $\rightarrow$ `"unhappiness"`).		 `unhappiness` = `un-` (prefix) + `happy` (root) + `-ness` (suffix).
2. Syntax Formal grammatical rules governing how words combine into phrases and sentences (Context-Free Grammars). $\text{S} \rightarrow \text{NP} \ \text{VP}$ (e.g., "The student [NP] solved the problem [VP]").
3. Semantics The study of literal, context-independent linguistic meaning and predicate-argument relationships (Semantic Roles: Agent, Action, Patient). "The dog chased the cat" $\rightarrow \text{Agent}=\text{dog}, \text{Action}=\text{chase}, \text{Patient}=\text{cat}$.
4. Pragmatics Meaning in real-world communicative context, discourse intent, and speaker implicature. "It is freezing in here!" $\implies$ Pragmatic command to close the window.
🧠 Memory Hook: S-S-P Paradigm
Syntax (Structure) $\longrightarrow$ Semantics (Sense / Meaning) $\longrightarrow$ Pragmatics (Situation / Context)

Topic 13 & 14: N-Gram Models, Laplace Smoothing & Perplexity [UPLOADED PYQ]

1. Bigram Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE): $$P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{\text{Count}(w_{i-1}, w_i)}{\text{Count}(w_{i-1})}$$
2. Laplace (Add-1) Smoothing Formula [UPLOADED PYQ]: $$P_{\text{Laplace}}(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{\text{Count}(w_{i-1}, w_i) + 1}{\text{Count}(w_{i-1}) + |V|}$$ Where $|V|$ is the total vocabulary size. Guarantees zero-probability events never crash sentence likelihoods!
3. Model Perplexity Metric ($PP$): $$PP(W) = P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_N)^{-\frac{1}{N}} = \sqrt[N]{\frac{1}{P(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_N)}}$$ Interpretation: The average branching factor of the language model (Lower Perplexity $\implies$ Higher Predictive Accuracy).
🏛️ [UPLOADED PYQ] Solved Problem: Bigram Probability with Laplace Smoothing

Given corpus counts: $\text{Count}(\text{"want", "to"}) = 600$, $\text{Count}(\text{"want"}) = 1200$, Vocabulary $|V| = 5000$.

  1. MLE Probability: $P_{\text{MLE}}(\text{"to"} \mid \text{"want"}) = \frac{600}{1200} = \mathbf{0.500}$.
  2. Laplace Smoothed Probability: $P_{\text{Laplace}}(\text{"to"} \mid \text{"want"}) = \frac{600 + 1}{1200 + 5000} = \frac{601}{6200} \approx \mathbf{0.0969}$.

Topic 15 & 16: HMM POS Tagging & The Viterbi Algorithm [UPLOADED PYQ]

A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) models Part-of-Speech tagging as finding the most probable hidden sequence of POS tags $T = \langle t_1, \dots, t_n \rangle$ given observed words $W = \langle w_1, \dots, w_n \rangle$:

$$\hat{T} = \arg\max_T P(T \mid W) = \arg\max_T \prod_{i=1}^n P(t_i \mid t_{i-1}) \cdot P(w_i \mid t_i)$$
The Viterbi Dynamic Programming Algorithm [UPLOADED PYQ]
$$\mathbf{V_t(j) = \max_{i=1}^N \Big[ V_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \Big] \cdot b_j(o_t)}$$ $$\mathbf{\text{Backpointer: } B_t(j) = \arg\max_{i=1}^N \Big[ V_{t-1}(i) \cdot a_{ij} \Big]}$$ Where $a_{ij} = P(t_j \mid t_i)$ is Tag Transition probability, and $b_j(o_t) = P(w_t \mid t_j)$ is Word Emission probability.

Topic 17 – 20: Syntactic Parsing (CFG, PCFG, CKY & Dependency Parsing) [UPLOADED PYQ]

Parsing Paradigm Grammar & Structural Formulation Key Advantages & Tradeoffs
1. Context-Free Grammar (CFG) $G = (V, \Sigma, R, S)$ where productions have form $A \rightarrow \alpha$ ($A \in V$). Recursive descent expands rules top-down. Precise; vulnerable to left-recursion infinite loops and combinatorial ambiguity explosions.
2. Probabilistic CFG (PCFG) [UPLOADED PYQ] Assigns probability to each rule: $P(A \rightarrow \alpha)$, where $\sum_\alpha P(A \rightarrow \alpha) = 1$. Parse probability is product of rule probabilities. Ranks multiple ambiguous parse trees and selects the mathematically most probable syntactic structure!
3. CKY Dynamic Programming [UPLOADED PYQ] Requires grammar in Chomsky Normal Form (CNF): $A \rightarrow B C$ or $A \rightarrow a$. Fills triangular table $O(n^3 \cdot |R|)$. Guaranteed polynomial $O(n^3)$ parsing time for all ambiguous sentences!
4. Dependency Parsing Connects words directly with labeled binary grammatical dependency links (e.g., $\text{nsubj}$, $\text{dobj}$, $\text{amod}$). Directly captures predicate-argument relationships; excellent for free-word-order languages.

Classification / Parsing Core Metrics: $$\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \quad \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \quad F_1\text{-Score} = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}$$ - Unlabeled Attachment Score (UAS): Percentage of words assigned the correct syntactic head governor. - Labeled Attachment Score (LAS): Percentage of words assigned both the correct head governor AND the correct dependency relation label.

Q1. [UPLOADED PYQ] Explain the working of HMM-based POS Tagging. Define Transition and Emission Probabilities. (10 Marks)
HMM treats POS tags as hidden Markov states generating observable surface words:
1. Transition Probability $P(t_i \mid t_{i-1})$: The probability of transitioning from tag $t_{i-1}$ to tag $t_i$: $P(t_i \mid t_{i-1}) = \frac{C(t_{i-1}, t_i)}{C(t_{i-1})}$.
2. Emission Probability $P(w_i \mid t_i)$: The probability that hidden tag $t_i$ emits the observable word $w_i$: $P(w_i \mid t_i) = \frac{C(t_i, w_i)}{C(t_i)}$.
3. Viterbi Algorithm: Computes the global optimal sequence of tags in $O(T \cdot N^2)$ time using dynamic programming lattice trellis!

Topic 25 – 29: Lexical Semantics, WordNet & WSD Algorithms

Lexical Relation Formal Definition Representative Example
1. SynonymyDifferent words expressing the identical or near-identical concept ($A \approx B$).`"large"` $\leftrightarrow$ `"big"`, `"automobile"` $\leftrightarrow$ `"car"`.
2. AntonymyWords expressing opposite semantic meanings along a spectrum ($A \leftrightarrow -A$).`"hot"` $\leftrightarrow$ `"cold"`, `"increase"` $\leftrightarrow$ `"decrease"`.
3. Hyponymy / Hypernymy$\text{IS-A}$ class-subclass hierarchical relationship ($A$ is a subtype of $B$).`"sparrow"` is a hyponym of `"bird"`; `"animal"` is a hypernym of `"dog"`.
4. Meronymy / Holonymy$\text{PART-OF}$ compositional relationship ($A$ is a physical component of $B$).`"wheel"` is a meronym of `"car"`; `"car"` is a holonym of `"wheel"`.
The Simplified Lesk WSD Algorithm (Lesk, 1986)
Given target ambiguous word $w$ in context sentence $C$:
  1. Retrieve all dictionary definitions (glosses) and usage examples for each synset sense $S_i$ of $w$ in WordNet.
  2. Compute the word token overlap score between the context sentence $C$ and the gloss/examples of sense $S_i$: $\text{Overlap}(S_i, C) = | \text{Words}(S_i.\text{gloss}) \cap \text{Words}(C) |$.
  3. Select the sense $\hat{S} = \arg\max_{S_i} \text{Overlap}(S_i, C)$ with the highest word overlap count.

Topic 30 – 32: Vector Space Models & TF-IDF Weighting [UPLOADED PYQ]

1. Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) [UPLOADED PYQ]: $$\text{TF}(t, d) = \frac{\text{Count}(t, d)}{\sum_{t' \in d} \text{Count}(t', d)}$$ $$\text{IDF}(t, D) = \log_{10} \left( \frac{N}{\text{DF}(t)} \right)$$ $$\mathbf{\text{TF-IDF}(t, d, D) = \text{TF}(t, d) \times \text{IDF}(t, D)}$$ Where $N$ is total documents in corpus, and $\text{DF}(t)$ is number of documents containing term $t$.
2. Vector Cosine Similarity Metric: $$\cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^d u_i v_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d u_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d v_i^2}}$$

Topic 33 – 35: Neural Word Embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText) [UPLOADED PYQ]

The Distributional Hypothesis (J.R. Firth, 1957)
"You shall know a word by the company it keeps!" Words appearing in similar contextual distributions acquire geometrically close spatial representations in $d$-dimensional embedding space.
Embedding Model Training Objective & Operational Mechanism Key Advantages & Tradeoffs
1. Word2Vec: CBOW [UPLOADED PYQ] Continuous Bag-of-Words: Predicts current center target word $w_t$ given surrounding context window words: $\arg\max \log P(w_t \mid w_{t-c}, \dots, w_{t+c})$. Fast to train; higher accuracy for frequent words; smooths over distributional noise.
2. Word2Vec: Skip-Gram [UPLOADED PYQ] Predicts surrounding context words given center word $w_t$: $\arg\max \sum \log P(w_{t+j} \mid w_t)$ using Negative Sampling. Superb for capturing subtle semantic nuances of rare words; slower to train.
3. GloVe Global Vectors (Stanford): Factorizes global log-co-occurrence matrix: $J = \sum f(X_{ij}) (w_i^T \tilde{w}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j - \log X_{ij})^2$. Combines global matrix factorization statistics with local context window advantages.
4. FastText (Meta AI) Represents each word as a bag of Character $n$-Grams (e.g., `""`, `""`). Seamlessly computes embeddings for Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) and morphologically complex words!

Topic 36 – 38: Sequential Neural Models (CNN, RNN, LSTM)

Neural Model Mathematical Formulation & Architecture Key Advantage & Failure Mode
1. CNN for NLP 1D convolution filters slide across embedding matrices capturing local $n$-gram phrase features: $c_i = f(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_{i:i+h-1} + b) \rightarrow$ Max-Pooling over time. Highly parallelizable; fails to model long-range sequential syntax dependencies.
2. Vanilla RNN Recurrent hidden state: $\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{hh} \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_{xh} \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_h)$. Unrolls across variable sentence lengths. Models sequential history; severely suffers from Vanishing and Exploding Gradients over $>10$ tokens.
3. LSTM (Hochreiter, 1997) Regulates information flow using 3 Gates + Constant Error Carousel Cell State $C_t$: Forget Gate $f_t$, Input Gate $i_t$, Output Gate $o_t$. Maintains long-range gradient highway over hundreds of tokens; sequential processing inhibits GPU scaling.
Complete LSTM Mathematical Gate Formulations: $$\text{Forget Gate: } \mathbf{f}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f)$$ $$\text{Input Gate: } \mathbf{i}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i), \quad \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_c [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_c)$$ $$\text{Cell State Update: } \mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{C}}_t$$ $$\text{Output Gate: } \mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_o [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o), \quad \mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{C}_t)$$

Topic 39 & 40: The Transformer Architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017)

1. Scaled Dot-Product Attention Equation: $$\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\left( \frac{\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \mathbf{V}$$ Where $\mathbf{Q}$ (Query), $\mathbf{K}$ (Key), and $\mathbf{V}$ (Value) are linear projections of input embeddings. Division by $\sqrt{d_k}$ prevents gradient saturation in large dimensions!
2. Multi-Head Attention (MHA): $$\text{MultiHead}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h) \mathbf{W}^O, \quad \text{head}_i = \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{W}_i^Q, \mathbf{K}\mathbf{W}_i^K, \mathbf{V}\mathbf{W}_i^V)$$

🧠 M4 Active Recall & Exam Questions

Q1. Explain the working of the Scaled Dot-Product Self-Attention mechanism in Transformers with a diagram. (10 Marks)
Self-attention calculates pairwise relational affinities between every pair of tokens in parallel:
1. Linear Projections: Input matrix $\mathbf{X}$ is multiplied by weight matrices $\mathbf{W}^Q, \mathbf{W}^K, \mathbf{W}^V$ to generate $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}$.
2. Score Calculation: Computes dot-product similarity matrix $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T$, reflecting how much each token attends to all other tokens.
3. Scaling & Normalization: Divides by $\sqrt{d_k}$ to stabilize gradients and applies row-wise $\text{softmax}(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^T}{\sqrt{d_k}})$.
4. Weighted Aggregation: Multiplies attention weights by value matrix $\mathbf{V}$ to output contextualized embeddings!

Topic 52 & 53: Ethics, Bias Mitigation & Model Explainability

Ethical / Interpretability Challenge Manifestation in Language Models Engineering Mitigation Method
1. Dataset & Representation Bias Stereotypical associations in word embeddings (e.g., $\text{"doctor"} - \text{"man"} + \text{"woman"} \approx \text{"nurse"}$). Counterfactual data augmentation, debiasing projection subspaces.
